Overall Inclusion criteria:
Original research studies involving individuals with cardiofaciocutaneous (CFC) syndrome or experimental models using CFC-associated RAS/MAPK mutations. Eligible study designs include randomized or non-randomized clinical studies, prospective or retrospective cohort studies, case-control studies, cross-sectional studies, registry-based analyses, observational studies, laboratory or animal experiments, and mechanistic investigations that generate new scientific knowledge relevant to the folder topic.

Overall AI sorting additions:
Include articles that directly involve CFC syndrome, individuals with CFC, CFC-associated germline RAS/MAPK variants, or experimental models using mutations that are clearly connected to CFC biology. Also include reviews, guidelines, diagnostic papers, and RASopathy-wide summaries when CFC is meaningfully discussed as part of the clinical, genetic, diagnostic, or management content. Exclude articles that are not CFC-specific or CFC-relevant. Exclude Noonan-only, Costello-only, Legius-only, NF1-only, cancer-only, somatic mutation-only, or broad RAS/MAPK pathway papers unless they include CFC cases/data, use a clearly CFC-associated mutation/model, or provide clinically useful CFC comparison within the RASopathy spectrum. Do not include an article only because it mentions RAS/MAPK, BRAF, MAP2K1, MAP2K2, KRAS, or RASopathy. The article must connect those findings to CFC syndrome, CFC-associated germline biology, CFC-relevant clinical management, or a meaningful RASopathy comparison that includes CFC. Make the exclusion screen slightly harsher than before: if full text shows CFC is only barely mentioned, appears only in a passing disease list, appears only in a figure/table label, appears only in references, or is not substantively discussed in the paper's aims/results/discussion, exclude it.

General AI sorting rules:
Choose one primary category. Add secondary categories only when the article substantially belongs in more than one section, not merely because it mentions multiple symptoms. Human sorting favors keeping CFC-relevant background material in the library: if an article is a broad review, clinical guideline, consensus statement, diagnostic overview, CFC index/diagnostic criteria paper, prenatal diagnosis overview, patient/family education article, historical overview, or case report/case series that does not present a clearly specialty-specific new finding, place it in General and Reviews rather than excluding it. Use specialty categories when the article contributes new specialty-specific evidence. Do not use Historical Articles or Conferences as category options.

Human-alignment examples from review:
Keep as General and Reviews when CFC is meaningfully discussed: CFC clinical features/management guidelines, CFC index/diagnostic criteria papers, prenatal diagnosis of RASopathies when CFC is discussed, older diagnostic papers written before gene discovery, and RASopathy reviews that compare CFC with related syndromes in the main text. Include RASopathy cohort papers in a specialty folder when CFC participants are actually included and the result is relevant, such as autoimmune findings that include CFC participants. Use Treatments as primary for therapeutic landscape or treatment review papers, with Cardiology or another organ system as additional when appropriate. Exclude papers where CFC is absent, only Noonan is discussed, or CFC is merely alluded to in one figure/table/reference without meaningful text discussion.

1. Allergy and Immunology
a. The Allergy and Immunology folder includes papers that examine immune dysregulation and hypersensitivity in cardiofaciocutaneous (CFC) syndrome and related RASopathies. These studies explore how mutations in the RAS/MAPK pathway alter immune cell signaling, leading to increased susceptibility to eczema, atopic dermatitis, and allergic rhinitis. Common symptoms discussed across these works include chronic skin inflammation, food or environmental allergies, and frequent respiratory infections due to impaired immune regulation. Collectively, the research highlights the connection between molecular signaling defects and the heightened allergic and inflammatory responses often observed in individuals with CFC.
b. Inclusion: Papers that directly investigate immune dysregulation, allergic disease, or inflammatory responses in individuals with confirmed or strongly suspected CFC syndrome, including studies using RAS/MAPK mutations specifically linked to CFC.
c. Exclusion: Studies focused solely on other RASopathies without CFC-specific data, generalized immunology papers without clinical relevance to CFC, or pathway studies lacking immune or allergy outcomes.
d. AI sorting addition: If eczema or skin findings are discussed primarily as a dermatologic phenotype rather than immune dysregulation, use Dermatology.

2. Cardiology
a. The cardiology folder includes research focused on cardiac involvement in cardiofaciocutaneous (CFC) syndrome, particularly studies examining how RAS/MAPK pathway mutations such as those in BRAF affect heart muscle development and function. These papers explore mechanisms leading to hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, where the heart muscle thickens and becomes less efficient at pumping blood. Common cardiac symptoms associated with CFC include ventricular hypertrophy, arrhythmias, outflow obstruction, and reduced contractile performance, often contributing to fatigue, shortness of breath, and exercise intolerance. Overall, this folder captures the intersection of molecular signaling defects and their clinical impact on cardiac structure and rhythm in CFC patients.
b. Inclusion: Research examining cardiac structure, function, or electrophysiology in CFC syndrome, including mechanistic studies using BRAF or MAPK mutations known to cause CFC.
c. Exclusion: Cardiac studies on Noonan, Costello, or other RASopathies without a CFC subgroup, or broad cardiomyopathy papers not tied to CFC relevant mutations.
d. AI sorting addition: If the paper is mainly about hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, heart function, MEK inhibition for cardiac disease, arrhythmia, or cardiac surveillance, prefer Cardiology. If it is a review/overview of RASopathy cardiac disease with meaningful CFC discussion but no new CFC-specific cardiac data, use General and Reviews with Cardiology as an additional category if appropriate. If the paper is mainly about treatment of heart disease, MEK inhibition, or therapeutic landscape, prefer Treatments with Cardiology as an additional category. If the article is mainly about molecular diagnosis rather than cardiac findings, use Genetics.

Common symptoms include dry, thickened, or hyperkeratotic skin, eczema-like rashes, keratosis pilaris, ulerythema ophryogenes, sparse or curly hair, and fragile nails. The research in this folder collectively explores the molecular and clinical basis of these dermatologic phenotypes, emphasizing how they serve as visible diagnostic clues for CFC and related syndromes.

d. AI sorting addition: If the paper is a dermatology review or viewpoint about RASopathies and includes meaningful CFC skin discussion, use General and Reviews with Dermatology as an additional category if appropriate. If a paper primarily discusses melanoma/cancer mechanisms rather than CFC skin phenotype, use Oncology or exclude if not CFC-specific.

7. General and Reviews
a. The General and Reviews folder includes foundational and overview papers that define, describe, and contextualize cardiofaciocutaneous (CFC) syndrome within the broader group of RASopathies. These studies often review clinical features, diagnostic criteria, and historical perspectives, comparing CFC with related conditions such as Noonan and Costello syndromes. Common symptoms discussed include distinctive facial morphology, cardiac defects, cutaneous abnormalities, growth delays, and developmental challenges. Collectively, this folder provides a comprehensive understanding of CFC's clinical variability, genetic basis, and evolving diagnostic framework, serving as a reference point for both specialized and general research on the syndrome.
b. Inclusion: Narrative reviews, systematic reviews, clinical guidelines, consensus statements, diagnostic criteria papers, CFC index papers, phenotype overviews, prenatal diagnosis overviews, historical descriptions, and case reports or case series that primarily describe individuals with CFC syndrome without presenting new experimental findings, novel genetic discoveries, or hypothesis-driven analyses. These articles serve as reference material and background information rather than original research. Also include RASopathy-wide reviews or overviews when they meaningfully discuss CFC, compare CFC with Noonan/Costello/other RASopathies, or provide useful CFC clinical context.
c. Exclusion: Original research studies, experimental investigations, mechanistic studies, clinical trials, observational studies, registry analyses, or case reports that provide clearly specialty-specific novel scientific findings, such as identification of a new mutation, new disease mechanism, or previously undescribed clinical manifestation, which should instead be categorized within the appropriate specialty folder. Exclude broad RAS/MAPK, cancer, or pathway papers that only mention CFC in passing and do not provide CFC clinical, genetic, diagnostic, or management context.
d. AI sorting addition: Reviews with nothing substantially new should go here even if they mention a specialty area. Do not exclude CFC-specific guidelines, CFC diagnostic criteria/index papers, or RASopathy reviews with meaningful CFC discussion simply because they are not original research.

8. Genetics
a. The Genetics folder includes papers that define the molecular and hereditary basis of cardiofaciocutaneous (CFC) syndrome and related RASopathies. These studies focus on how mutations in the RAS/MAPK signaling pathway particularly in genes such as BRAF, MAP2K1, MAP2K2, KRAS, and RAF1 disrupt normal cell growth and differentiation, leading to the multisystem features characteristic of CFC. Common topics include genotype-phenotype correlations, mutation spectrum analysis, and comparative studies distinguishing CFC from Noonan and Costello syndromes. Collectively, this folder highlights the genetic diversity underlying CFC and the diagnostic importance of molecular testing for accurate classification and management.
b. Inclusion: Studies that identify, characterize, or validate pathogenic variants associated with CFC syndrome through molecular genetic testing, whole-genome sequencing, whole-exome sequencing, targeted sequencing, bioinformatic analyses, genotype-phenotype correlation studies, or functional validation of novel variants. Papers should contribute new knowledge that would be relevant to genetic diagnosis, counseling, variant interpretation, or disease classification.
c. Exclusion: Studies limited to somatic or tumor-specific mutations without relevance to inherited CFC syndrome, papers that merely report previously established pathogenic variants without additional genetic insight, or general molecular pathway studies not directly related to CFC-associated germline mutations or clinical genetics.
d. AI sorting addition: If a paper primarily identifies or validates a CFC pathogenic variant, genotype-phenotype relationship, inheritance pattern, or variant function for clinical genetics, prefer Genetics even if it also describes clinical features. If a clinical specialty finding is the clear main contribution and genetics is only background, use the specialty folder.
